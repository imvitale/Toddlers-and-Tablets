Small swaps, big wins

A simple guide to screen time for parents of kids under 5.

See the 3 swaps

The first five years are when kids build their sleep habits, language and social skills. Research from Canada and other countries shows that time on phones and tablets during these years is linked to shorter sleep and slower speech and social development.

You're not a bad parent for handing over the phone. We've all done it in the grocery line.

Sleep

Toddlers who use touchscreens daily tend to sleep less at night.

Speech

At 18 months, every extra 30 minutes a day on a handheld device was linked to more than double the odds of delayed talking.

School

Kindergarteners with more than 1 hour of screen time a day were more likely to struggle with social skills, language and communication.

Mood

In teens and young adults, problematic smartphone use was linked to about three times the odds of depression and anxiety.

3 easy swaps

Small changes that fit into everyday family life.

Screen-free hour before bed

Screens close to bedtime are linked to toddlers getting less sleep at night. Turning devices off an hour before bed gives your child time to wind down.

Try this: Make it part of the bedtime routine. Bath, pajamas, then a story or two together instead of a show.

Device-free meals

Young children learn to talk through back-and-forth conversation with the adults around them. Mealtimes are one of the easiest daily chances for that.

Try this: Keep phones and tablets away from the table and ask, "What was the best part of your day?" Even short answers count as practice.

Watch together

When screens are used, watching with your child turns it into a shared activity instead of time spent alone with a device.

Try this: Sit with them, point to what's happening on the screen and ask questions like "What do you think happens next?"

Links, not proof

Most of these studies are correlational. That means they show screens and outcomes like sleep or speech go together, but they can't prove that screens are the cause. Still, studies using different methods in different places keep pointing in the same direction. That is why Canada's guideline exists, and why small changes at home are worth trying.
